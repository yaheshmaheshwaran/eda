Introduction

Climate change is the defining challenge of our era. At its core lies the relentless rise of carbon dioxide (CO₂) — the primary greenhouse gas responsible for trapping heat in Earth's atmosphere. Since the Industrial Revolution, human activities — burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and industrial production — have released billions of tonnes of CO₂ annually.

This report presents a comprehensive Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) of global CO₂ emissions from 1950 to 2022. Through 10 carefully crafted visualizations and statistical analyses, we investigate: which countries emit the most, how emissions evolved over time, which fuel sources drive the crisis, and whether economic development inevitably leads to higher carbon output.

Research Questions: How have global CO₂ emissions changed since 1950? Which nations are most responsible — in absolute terms, per-capita terms, and historically? Is there a relationship between wealth and carbon output?

Methodology

3.1 Data Preprocessing

The raw dataset required several preprocessing steps before analysis:

  • ✅ Filtering: Removed aggregate rows (World, continents, income groups) to retain only country-level data
  • ✅ Year Scoping: Focused on 1950–2022 where data density is highest
  • ✅ Missing Values: Applied forward-fill then back-fill within each country group to handle minor gaps
  • ✅ Feature Engineering: Created GDP per capita, decade groupings, and derived growth metrics
  • ✅ Snapshot Dataset: Extracted 2022 (latest year) as a cross-sectional slice for comparative analysis

Key Findings

  • 1
    Global CO₂ grew by ~420% from 1950 (8.5 Gt) to 2022 (37.1 Gt), an acceleration driven primarily by post-WWII industrialisation in Asia.
  • 2
    China dominates current emissions at ~31% of global CO₂ (11.4 Gt in 2022), driven by coal-powered manufacturing and infrastructure growth.
  • 3
    Per-capita fairness tells a different story: Qatar (~35 t/person) and UAE (~22 t/person) far exceed China (8.1 t) or India (1.9 t), exposing inequality in carbon footprints.
  • 4
    Coal is still king: Despite growth in renewables, coal accounts for ~40% of global energy CO₂ in 2022 — largely driven by China, India, and the USA.
  • 5
    USA leads historical responsibility with ~25% of all cumulative CO₂ since 1750, highlighting the intergenerational fairness debate in climate negotiations.
  • 6
    China's CO₂ surged 15× from 760 Mt (1950) to 11,397 Mt (2022) — the steepest industrialisation-driven growth of any nation in history.
  • 7
    India's per-capita footprint remains low (1.9 t/person vs global avg 4.7 t), underscoring that development equity must be central to climate policy.
  • 8
    GDP strongly correlates with CO₂ per capita (logarithmic R² ≈ 0.55), but outliers like Norway and Switzerland prove high income need not mean high emissions.
  • 9
    Growth is decelerating post-2015: The Paris Agreement coincides with a measurable flattening of the global emissions curve, partly due to renewable energy reaching cost competitiveness.
  • 10
    Cement and flaring are emerging sources: Both grew faster than coal or oil in the 2010–2022 period, driven by construction booms in developing nations.

Conclusion

This EDA reveals that global CO₂ emissions have grown at an alarming rate since 1950, threatening to breach the critical 1.5°C warming threshold set by the Paris Agreement. The picture is complex: while China leads in absolute current emissions, the United States bears the greatest historical responsibility. Meanwhile, nations like India — with a per-capita footprint a fraction of the global average — face the moral challenge of pursuing economic development without replicating the carbon-intensive path of industrialised nations.

The data shows that economic prosperity and low emissions are not mutually exclusive — countries like Norway and Switzerland demonstrate that high GDP can coexist with moderate carbon footprints. This is driven by policies favouring renewable energy, energy efficiency, and carbon pricing.

The slight deceleration in emission growth post-2015 offers cautious optimism. However, the trajectory remains far from what climate science demands. A rapid, equitable, and globally coordinated transition away from fossil fuels — particularly coal — remains the central challenge of our generation.

Policy Implication: Effective climate action requires differentiated responsibilities — acknowledging both current emission levels and historical contributions — while ensuring developing nations can pursue sustainable growth pathways.
